	\section{Original Study}
	\noindent I replicate the following study: "Genschel, P., Limberg, J., \& Seelkopf, L. (2024). Revenue, Redistribution, and the Rise and Fall of Inheritance Taxation. Comparative Political Studies, 57(9), 1475-1505." 

\vspace{0.3cm}
	
	\subsection{Background and Research Question}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{Research Question:} Why do many countries repeal the inheritance tax after having previously introduced it? 
		
		\item \textbf{Limitations of Existing Literature:} Conventional public policy research suggests that once policies are implemented, they tend to persist due to institutional inertia and entrenched interests. However, inheritance taxation represents a notable exception, having experienced a widespread decline since the mid-20th century.
		
		\item \textbf{Authors' Perspective:} The authors argue that the “rise and fall” of the inheritance tax is better understood through changes in its fiscal role within the state. In particular, they emphasize that its trajectory is primarily driven by transformations in its revenue function, rather than by purely political or ideological factors such as democratic pressure or elite capture.
	\end{itemize}
	
	\subsection{Hypotheses}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{Revenue Hypothesis:} The likelihood of inheritance tax repeal increases as governments adopt and expand more efficient and stable revenue instruments, such as income taxes and Value Added Tax (VAT). As these alternative sources grow, the fiscal necessity of inheritance taxation declines, making it more politically expendable.
		
		\item \textbf{Redistribution Hypothesis:} Once the fiscal importance of inheritance taxation weakens, its survival depends on redistributive considerations. In this context, democratic regimes—where redistributive demands are more politically salient—are expected to be less likely to repeal the tax than non-democratic regimes.
	\end{itemize}
	
	\subsection{Data and Method}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{Data:} The authors use a novel dataset tracking inheritance tax introductions and repeals for a global sample of 87 countries over approximately two centuries (1759–2015).
		
		\item \textbf{Method:} The study utilizes a Binary Time-Series Cross-Section (BTSCS) dataset, where country-years are observed until the repeal of the inheritance tax.
		
		\item \textbf{Model:} The analysis is conducted using logistic regression models estimated via maximum likelihood. To account for temporal dependence in BTSCS data, a cubic polynomial approximation $(t,t^2,t^3)$ is included to model time at risk.
	\end{itemize}

	\section{Data}
	\noindent The main analysis is conducted using the \texttt{merge\_full\_2} dataset, which is a Binary Time-Series Cross-Section (BTSCS) dataset tracking countries at risk of repealing their inheritance tax over time.
	\vspace{0.3cm}
	\subsection{Outcome Variable}
	\noindent Inheritance tax repeal (y), coded as:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Inheritance tax is permanently repealed: 1
		\item Inheritance tax is maintained (or censored): 0
	\end{itemize}
		\vspace{0.3cm}
	\subsection{Input Variables}
	\subsubsection{Key Independent Variables:}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{Major modern taxes (other\_tax):} An index measuring the introduction of income and consumption taxes, coded as 0 (neither), 1 (either), or 2 (both).
		\item \textbf{Democracy (democracy):} A binary indicator of democratic regime type.
	\end{itemize}
	
	\subsubsection{Control Variables:}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{Fiscal/Economic:} Includes indicators for recent war (war\_previous), recent economic recession (recession\_prev\_5), and social policy introduction (wfs\_intro\_previous).
		\item \textbf{Demographic/Structural:} Includes logged population for tax competition \texttt{(log(population\_new)}, logged GDP per capita \texttt{(log\_gdp\_cap)}, life expectancy \texttt{(e\_pelifeex)}, and logged time since the tax was introduced \texttt{(log(time\_since\_intro))}.
		\item \textbf{Temporal Dependence ($t, t2, t3$): }A cubic polynomial approximation of time at risk to account for temporal dependence in the BTSCS data.
	\end{itemize}

	\subsection{Main Regression Model (Table 1)}
	To test their hypotheses on tax repeal, the authors estimate a series of logistic regression models using maximum likelihood estimation. To account for temporal dependence in the Binary Time-Series Cross-Section (BTSCS) data, they include a cubic polynomial approximation (t, t2, t3).

	\section{My Contribution}
	\noindent While the original model provides valuable insights, the interpretation of log-odds coefficients remains substantively unintuitive. For instance, the coefficient for \texttt{Major modern taxes} (1.098) indicates that a one-unit increase in the index raises the log-odds of repeal, holding other variables constant. While this conveys direction and statistical significance, it does not provide an intuitive sense of how the probability of repeal changes in practice.
	
	Moreover, the original model assumes that the effects of fiscal capacity and democracy are strictly additive. This may be overly restrictive, as democratic institutions could plausibly moderate the relationship between revenue conditions and policy outcomes. 
	
	Therefore, in this section, I extend the original model by incorporating an interaction term and translating the results into predicted probabilities, providing a more intuitive and theoretically nuanced interpretation.
	
	\subsection{The Interaction Hypothesis}
	\noindent I argue that we can formulate a theoretical interaction hypothesis:
	\begin{hypothesis} 
		The impact of modern taxes on the likelihood of inheritance tax repeal varies depending on the level of democracy. 
	\end{hypothesis}
	\noindent According to the authors, democracies resist inheritance tax repeals because they protect the redistributive interests of the lower classes. If this is true, even when a state achieves "revenue redundancy" by adopting efficient modern taxes, democratic institutions should resist the fiscal pressure to repeal the inheritance tax more strongly than autocracies. Therefore, the positive effect of the \texttt{other\_tax} variable should be weaker in democratic regimes. 
	
	\subsection{Statistical Concept Application and Visualization}
	To test this hypothesis, I apply the concepts learned in Applied Stats II by adding an interaction term \texttt{(other\_tax * democracy)} to the original logistic regression model. Furthermore, to move beyond the unintuitive log-odds interpretation, I use the \texttt{marginaleffects} package in R. By plotting the predicted probability S-curves, I visually demonstrate how the probability of an inheritance tax repeal behaves differently for democracies versus autocracies as the modern tax index increases.
	\subsubsection{Table 2: Interaction Model}
	\begin{lstlisting}[language=R]
		# Fit interaction model: other_tax * democracy
		model_int <- glm(y ~ other_tax * democracy + war_previous + 
		recession_prev_5 + wfs_intro_previous + 
		log(time_since_intro) + log_gdp_cap + e_pelifeex + 
		log(population_new) + t + t2 + t3,
		data = merge_full_2, family = binomial(link = "logit"))
	\end{lstlisting}
	
	\input{data_SK/Table2.tex}
	\noindent Note: Table 2 shows that the interaction term Major Modern Taxes x Democracy is negative (-0.417), aligning with the theoretical expectation that democratic institutions attenuate the effect of revenue redundancy. However, the interaction term does not achieve statistical significance at conventional levels, suggesting that the empirical evidence for this moderating effect remains inconclusive.
	
	\vspace{0.5cm}
	\subsubsection{Figure 7: Predicted Probability S-Curves}
	\noindent As visualized in Figure 7, when the modern tax index increases, the predicted probability of an inheritance tax repeal rises sharply in autocracies (red line). In contrast, in democratic regimes (blue line), this curve is significantly flattened, remaining below a 0.01 probability even at the highest levels of the modern tax index. 
	
	\begin{lstlisting}[language=R]
		# Generate Predicted Probability S-Curves
		fig7 <- plot_predictions(model_int, condition = c("other_tax", "democracy")) +
		theme_bw() +
		labs(title = "Predicted Probability of Inheritance Tax Repeal",
		x = "Major Modern Taxes Index", y = "Predicted Probability") +
		scale_color_manual(values = c("red", "blue"), labels = c("Autocracy", "Democracy"))
	\end{lstlisting}

	\noindent Although the interaction term itself is not statistically significant—likely due to the overall rarity of repeal events (only 40 out of 6,087 observations)—the graphical representation provides suggestive visual evidence consistent with the theoretical expectation. It effectively demonstrates that democratic institutions serve as a powerful buffer against the fiscal pressures to repeal the inheritance tax, adding a crucial and intuitive nuance to the original authors' additive claims.

	\section{Conclusion}
	
	\noindent This replication study confirms the main findings of Genschel, Limberg, and Seelkopf (2024), demonstrating that the likelihood of inheritance tax repeal is strongly associated with changes in fiscal structure, particularly the expansion of more efficient revenue sources. The replicated results closely align with the original estimates, reinforcing the robustness and reproducibility of the authors’ core arguments.
	
	Building on this, the extension introduced in this paper highlights the potential moderating role of democratic institutions. While the interaction model suggests that democracies may attenuate the effect of revenue redundancy on tax repeal, the lack of statistical significance indicates that this relationship remains suggestive rather than conclusive.
	
	Overall, this analysis illustrates both the strengths and limitations of the original model. While it provides a compelling explanation centered on fiscal capacity, further research could explore more complex interactions between political institutions and economic conditions. In particular, future work may benefit from addressing the rarity of repeal events and employing alternative estimation strategies to better capture such dynamics.
	
	\vspace{0.3cm}
	
	\noindent Taken together, this study demonstrates not only the empirical robustness of the original findings but also the value of extending replication exercises to generate new theoretical and methodological insights.
